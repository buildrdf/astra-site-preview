Chara Karakas and the Atmakaraka: The Jaimini System Explained

One word, two systems — a fixed set from Parashara and a moving one from Jaimini.

The short answer

A karaka is a significator, a graha that stands for a life topic. Parashari karakas are fixed forever — the Sun always signifies the father, Venus always the spouse. Jaimini's chara (movable) karakas are ranked chart by chart, by degree: whichever graha has travelled furthest through its sign becomes the Atmakaraka, the chart's central figure.

One word, two traditions

Karaka, defined

Karakaa significator — a graha standing for a particular topic, relationship, or person in a chart

Vedic astrology uses the word karaka for two genuinely different ideas, and most confusion about karakas comes from not knowing which one is on the table. One system, attributed to the sage Parashara, assigns each graha a fixed topic that never changes from chart to chart. The other, from the sage Jaimini, ranks the grahas differently in every single chart, by how far each has travelled through its own sign. Knowing which system a statement belongs to resolves almost every apparent contradiction you will run into.

Fixed karakas: the Parashari significators

In the Parashara tradition — the older, more widely taught branch of Vedic astrology — each of the seven classical grahas permanently signifies certain people and topics, regardless of which chart you are reading. These assignments never move:

These are the karakas most beginner material teaches first, because they require nothing beyond knowing which graha is which — no degree, no ranking, no chart-specific calculation. When a classical text says "Jupiter is the karaka for children," it is speaking in this fixed, Parashari sense.

Movable karakas: the Jaimini chara karakas

How the ranking works

The Jaimini school takes a completely different approach. Instead of fixed roles, it ranks the seven classical grahas by degree — literally, how many degrees each has moved through the sign it currently occupies. The graha with the highest degree in its sign takes the first and most important role, the next-highest takes the second role, and so on down the line. Because every chart places its grahas at different degrees, this ranking is different for every single person; it is chara, movable, rather than fixed.

  1. List the degree-within-sign for each of the seven classical grahas (0° to 30°)
  2. Rank them from highest degree to lowest
  3. Assign the seven roles in that order, highest degree first

The seven roles, in order

  • Atmakaraka — the self; traditionally read as the chart's central, protagonist figure
  • Amatyakaraka — counsel and career; the minister to the Atmakaraka's king
  • Bhratrikaraka — siblings and close mentors
  • Matrikaraka — the mother
  • Putrakaraka — children
  • Gnatikaraka — rivals and obstacles
  • Darakaraka — spouse and partnership; traditionally the lowest degree of all

The Atmakaraka: why it matters most

Of the seven, the Atmakaraka gets by far the most attention in Jaimini practice. It is the graha sitting at the highest degree in its sign, and classical Jaimini technique reads it as the chart's lead actor — the graha whose concerns, in the plainest sense, this incarnation is most about. Where a Parashari reading might build a story from a lagna lord or a strong yogakaraka, a Jaimini reading often starts from the Atmakaraka and asks what its sign, house, and nakshatra add to that central theme.

Darakaraka: the Jaimini approach to marriage

The Darakaraka — literally the lowest-degree graha of the set — is Jaimini's own significator for spouse and partnership, distinct from Venus's fixed Parashari role as karaka for the spouse. The two do not have to be the same graha in a given chart, and classical practice reads them as complementary lines of evidence about relationships rather than as rivals.

Seven karakas or eight? A real disagreement

Whether Rahu joins the ranking as an eighth karaka is a genuine, long-standing disagreement between Jaimini practitioners, not a mistake to be corrected. Both the seven-karaka and eight-karaka schemes are attested in classical and later texts.

The classical seven-karaka scheme uses only the Sun through Saturn — the seven grahas with a physical body, excluding the two lunar nodes. A widely practiced variant scheme adds Rahu as an eighth karaka, ranked alongside the seven and usually displacing whichever planet would otherwise be Gnatikaraka, since Rahu's own significations already sit close to that role. Neither position is wrong within its own lineage; if you compare Atmakaraka results across two pieces of software, checking which scheme each uses is usually the first thing to check.

A worked example

Suppose a chart holds these degrees within sign: Saturn at 27°, Venus at 19°, the Moon at 14°, Mercury at 9°, Mars at 6°, Jupiter at 4°, and the Sun at 2°. Ranking by degree alone — highest to lowest — gives Saturn the Atmakaraka role, Venus the Amatyakaraka, the Moon the Bhratrikaraka, Mercury the Matrikaraka, Mars the Putrakaraka, Jupiter the Gnatikaraka, and the Sun, sitting lowest, the Darakaraka.

Notice what this ranking does not depend on: which sign each graha sits in, which house it occupies, or its dignity. Two charts can share an identical Atmakaraka graha while looking nothing alike in sign, house, or overall strength — the ranking only cares about relative degree within sign, nothing else. This is exactly why classical Jaimini technique treats the Atmakaraka's own sign and house as the next thing worth reading, once the ranking itself is settled.

A common mistake: confusing the two systems

A frequent error in casual reading is quoting a Parashari significance ("Venus is the karaka for marriage") and a Jaimini one ("the Darakaraka shows the spouse") as though they were the same claim about the same graha. They are two independent lines of reasoning from two different schools, and a chart reader should be able to name which one is in use at any given moment.

Because both systems use the word karaka, a sentence like "Jupiter is your karaka" is genuinely ambiguous without more context: fixed Parashari role, or a chart-specific Jaimini rank? Precise writing — and a well-built app — should always say which is meant, and this app labels each explicitly rather than leaving the reader to guess.

Reading the two systems together

A well-rounded chart reading rarely picks one karaka system and discards the other. The fixed Parashari karakas are the backbone of most house-and-lordship analysis — asking, for instance, whether the karaka for children (Jupiter) is well placed relative to the 5th house. The Jaimini chara karakas add a second, chart-specific lens on top, especially the Atmakaraka's own placement, which many practitioners treat as one of the more individualizing details a chart offers, precisely because it depends on exact degree rather than a fixed assignment shared by everyone with the same sign.

In practice, a thorough reading might use the fixed karakas to answer "what does this topic generally look like," and the chara karakas — especially the Atmakaraka and Darakaraka — to answer "what is distinctly true about this person's version of that topic." Neither replaces the other; they are different-grained tools built by different schools, centuries apart, and classical Vedic practice has long since folded both into a single working method rather than forcing a choice between them.

Open your grahas' advanced details and find the one sitting at the highest degree within its sign. That is your Atmakaraka in the Jaimini reading — the lead role in your own chart, not a role everyone shares. →

Questions people ask

What is Atmakaraka in astrology?

The Atmakaraka is the graha that has travelled the furthest through its own sign (highest degree) among the classical seven, in the Jaimini system. Traditionally read as the chart's central, protagonist figure, it changes from person to person because it depends on each graha's exact degree, not a fixed assignment.

What is the difference between karaka and chara karaka?

A karaka in the general (Parashari) sense is a fixed significator — the Sun always represents the father, for instance, in every chart. A chara karaka (Jaimini) is movable: which graha plays which of the seven roles, starting with Atmakaraka, is ranked freshly for every chart by degree.

Is Rahu one of the karakas?

Depends on the scheme. The classical seven-karaka scheme uses only the Sun through Saturn. A widely used eight-karaka variant adds Rahu to the ranking. Both are attested in Jaimini practice; this is a genuine disagreement between traditions, not an error.

What is Darakaraka and how is it different from Venus?

Darakaraka is the Jaimini chara karaka for spouse and partnership — the graha at the lowest degree among the set for that chart. Venus is the fixed Parashari karaka for spouse in every chart. The two systems can point to different grahas in the same chart, and classical readers treat both as evidence rather than picking one as correct.

How do you calculate the chara karakas?

List each of the seven classical grahas' degree within its own sign (0° to 30°, ignoring which sign it is). Rank them from highest degree to lowest. Assign the seven roles — Atmakaraka down to Darakaraka — in that order, highest first.

Can two people have the same Atmakaraka?

Yes — two charts can easily share the same graha as Atmakaraka, since it only depends on which graha has the highest degree, not the exact degree itself. What is far less likely to match is the exact degree and sign that graha sits in, which is what gives each chart's Atmakaraka its individual flavor.

Panchang Explained: The Five Limbs of the Vedic Day

Where a birth chart reads a person, a panchang reads a day — the same sky, for everyone.

The short answer

A panchang is the traditional Indian almanac, and the word means five limbs: vara (weekday), tithi (lunar day), nakshatra (the Moon's lunar mansion), yoga (a Sun-Moon combination), and karana (half a tithi). Together they describe a single day's sky rather than one person's birth moment, and they are what decide when festivals and auspicious timings fall.

A birth chart describes a person; a panchang describes a day

A birth chart is cast once, for one moment and one place, and read for the rest of a life. A panchang is different: it is recalculated every day, for everywhere at once, and it describes that day rather than any particular person. It is the oldest and most everyday application of Indian astronomy — the reason a festival lands on the date it does, year after year, on a lunar rather than a purely solar calendar.

The five limbs, one at a time

All five limbs are pure arithmetic on the longitudes of the Sun and Moon for that day and place. Nothing here is invented or interpreted at the level of the calculation; the five numbers are simply read off the sky.

Tithi — the lunar day

A tithi is one thirtieth of the Moon's monthly lap away from the Sun and back: specifically, 12° of elongation between the Moon and the Sun. Because the Moon moves faster than the Sun along the ecliptic, it slowly pulls ahead over the course of a month, and each 12° of that separation is one tithi. Thirty tithis make a full lunar month — fifteen in the waxing half (shukla paksha), fifteen in the waning half (krishna paksha) — and they are what festival dates are actually set by, not the solar calendar date.

Tithia lunar day: 12° of angular distance between the Moon and the Sun, thirty of which make one lunar month

Vara and nakshatra — the weekday and the Moon's lunar mansion

Vara is simply the weekday, and each one is ruled by a graha: Sunday the Sun, Monday the Moon, then Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn. Nakshatra is the lunar mansion — one of twenty-seven belts of sky — that the Moon occupies on a given day, the same division used for a birth nakshatra, just read for today's Moon instead of the Moon you were born under.

Yoga — the fifth limb's namesake collision

The panchang's own yoga is unrelated to the planetary-combination yoga discussed elsewhere in this series — it shares only the word. It is found by adding the Sun's and Moon's sidereal longitudes together and dividing the result into twenty-seven equal parts, each with its own traditional name and character, from Vishkambha through Vaidhriti. It is the least well known of the five limbs outside specialist use, but it is calculated and named for every single day.

Karana — half a tithi

A karana is exactly half of a tithi, so two karanas make each lunar day and there are sixty half-tithis across a full lunar month. Eleven karanas are named in total: seven “movable” ones that repeat in a cycle through most of the month, and four fixed ones — Shakuni, Chatushpada, Naga, and Kimstughna — that each occur exactly once, anchored to the very start or end of the lunar month.

Hora — the planetary hour

The hora divides each day into twenty-four planetary hours and hands them out in order of a graha's apparent slowness across the sky: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon, then the cycle repeats. A day takes its name from whichever graha rules its very first hour, counted from sunrise.

Horaa planetary hour — one of twenty-four in a day, each ruled by a graha in a fixed order of apparent slowness

That order also explains why the week runs Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, and onward in the sequence it does. Twenty-four hours is three more than three full turns of the seven-graha cycle, so each new day begins three places further along than the last one did. Three places on from the Sun is the Moon — which is exactly why Sunday is followed by Monday, and the whole weekly order falls out the same way, hour by hour, with no separate rule needed.

Confusingly, "hora" is also the name of the D2 divisional chart, which halves each sign of the birth chart in two. Same word, two unrelated instruments — one measures time within a day, the other reslices a birth longitude. Neither one turns into the other.

Unequal hours: why a panchang needs your place as well as your date

The twenty-four horas are not sixty minutes each. The classical, older way of keeping time divides sunrise to sunset into twelve equal parts, and sunset to the next sunrise into another twelve — so an hour runs longer during a long summer day and shorter in winter, and daytime and night-time hours are rarely the same length as each other. That is also why a panchang cannot be computed from a date alone; it needs a specific place, because sunrise and sunset times shift with location.

Choghadiya, and what any of this is actually good for

The classical use of a panchang is choosing when to begin something, never predicting what will happen. An hour ruled by Mercury is traditionally favoured for correspondence and negotiation; one ruled by Jupiter for teaching, counsel, or anything meant to grow over time. The choghadiya table divides the same day a second, independent way, into eight segments each marked favourable or set aside, and is popularly used alongside the hora for the same purpose.

None of it describes a guaranteed outcome, and no limb of the panchang should be read as a warning. Treat an unfavourable window the way you would treat any ordinary piece of scheduling advice — a reason to wait an hour, not a reason to be anxious about it.

Questions people ask

What is a panchang in Vedic astrology?

A panchang is the traditional Indian almanac, calculated fresh for each day and place rather than for one person's birth. Its name means "five limbs": vara (weekday), tithi (lunar day), nakshatra (the Moon's lunar mansion for the day), yoga (a Sun-Moon combination), and karana (half a tithi). It is the basis for festival dates and traditional auspicious-timing choices.

What is a tithi?

A tithi is a lunar day: 12° of angular separation between the Moon and the Sun along the ecliptic. Because the Moon moves faster than the Sun, this separation grows steadily across a month, producing thirty tithis in total — fifteen waxing and fifteen waning. Festival dates are set by tithi, which is why they shift against the solar calendar every year.

How many karanas are there, and why does the count seem to change?

There are eleven named karanas across sixty half-tithis in a lunar month. Seven of them are "movable" and repeat in a cycle through most of the month, which is why the same few names recur; four are fixed and each occurs exactly once, anchored to the very beginning or end of the lunar month.

What is a hora, and is it the same as the D2 chart?

Hora most commonly means a planetary hour: one of twenty-four divisions of a day, each ruled by a graha in a fixed order of apparent slowness starting from Saturn. It shares its name with the D2 divisional chart, which instead halves each sign of a birth chart, but the two are unrelated calculations that happen to share one Sanskrit word.

Why does the week go Sunday, Monday, Tuesday in that particular order?

The order comes directly from the planetary-hour sequence. A day has twenty-four hours, which is three more than three full turns through the seven-graha cycle, so each new day's first hour lands three places further along than the previous day's did. Three places on from the Sun is the Moon, which is why Sunday is followed by Monday, and so on around the week.

What is choghadiya?

Choghadiya is a separate way of dividing the same day, into eight segments rather than twenty-four planetary hours, each marked traditionally favourable or set aside for new undertakings. It is used alongside the hora for the same broad purpose: choosing a rough window to begin something, not for predicting what will happen.

Does a panchang predict what will happen on a given day?

No. Its traditional use is timing — suggesting when to begin something, not forecasting outcomes. An unfavourable hour or karana is traditionally treated as a reason to wait rather than as a warning of misfortune, and none of the five limbs describes a certain result for anyone reading them.

How Do Planetary Transits (Gochara) Work in Vedic Astrology?

Your chart is fixed. The sky keeps moving — and tradition counts that movement from your Moon.

The short answer

Gochara is the Vedic technique of reading current planetary positions against your natal chart. Unlike Western transit astrology, gochara is traditionally counted from your natal Moon sign, not your ascendant, because the Moon represents the mind moving through changing skies. Each transiting planet is graded by which house it currently occupies counted from that Moon.

Counted From the Moon, Not the Ascendant

Gocharatransit — the current motion of grahas (planets) read against the birth chart

Your birth chart is fixed the moment you're born, but the sky keeps moving, and gochara is the reading of that ongoing motion against your personal chart. Here Vedic practice differs plainly from Western transit astrology: gochara is traditionally counted from your natal Moon sign, not your ascendant. The Moon represents the mind, and transits describe the weather the mind moves through, day after day and year after year.

How a Transit Is Graded

Every Planet Keeps Its Own Clock

Varshaphalthe annual chart, cast for the Sun's exact return to its birth position each year

Because each planet laps the zodiac at its own speed, each returns to its own birth position on a roughly fixed schedule, and tradition treats these returns as natural chapter marks. The Sun returns every birthday, the moment for which almanac-makers traditionally cast the varshaphal, or year-ahead chart. Jupiter returns to its natal sign roughly every twelve years, traditionally read as a cycle of growth and counsel. The lunar nodes complete their backward lap of the zodiac in about eighteen and a half years.

Sade Sati, Calmly

Sade sati is Saturn's transit through the 12th, 1st and 2nd houses counted from your natal Moon — about seven and a half years altogether, arriving once roughly every thirty years as Saturn completes its own orbit. Popular astrology has turned this into something of a bogeyman. The more measured, traditional reading treats it as a long season of pruning, where commitments are tested and what is inessential tends to fall away; many people look back on their sade sati as the period that shaped them rather than harmed them.

The three phases have names of their own: Rising as Saturn transits the 12th from the Moon, Peak through the 1st, and Setting through the 2nd. None of this describes a fixed, guaranteed outcome — classical texts describe it as a difficult season to be met with patience, not as a curse to be feared.

Chandrashtama: The Monthly Low

Chandrashtamathe transiting Moon in the 8th house from your natal Moon — about two days each month

Chandrashtama is the monthly counterpart to sade sati's yearly rhythm: brief, roughly two days out of every twenty-seven or so, as the fast-moving Moon passes the 8th house from your natal Moon. Tradition reads it as a low-energy stretch better suited to finishing tasks than starting new ones. It passes on its own within a couple of days, and needs nothing more than a lighter schedule while it lasts.

Tara Bala: A Faster Daily Layer

Tara Balaa nine-category daily rating found by counting from your birth nakshatra (lunar constellation) to today's transiting nakshatra

Where gochara reads planet-by-planet, tara bala grades each single day. Count inclusively from your birth nakshatra to the Moon's current nakshatra, then reduce that count to its position in a repeating nine-fold cycle. The result is a running daily rating traditionally used for choosing the timing of small decisions, refreshed roughly every day as the Moon moves into a new nakshatra about every twenty-seven hours.

Worked Example: Reading a Jupiter Transit

Suppose your natal Moon sits in Taurus, and Jupiter is currently transiting Virgo — the 5th house counted from Taurus. The human reading: a season traditionally associated with opportunities in learning, creative projects or matters concerning children. The technical basis underneath that reading is simply the house count — Virgo sitting five signs on from your natal Moon sign of Taurus, nothing more mystical than counting.

Common Mistakes Reading Transits

  • Counting gochara from the ascendant out of habit, when the classical technique specifically starts from the natal Moon
  • Treating sade sati or chandrashtama as guaranteed misfortune rather than a named, temporary season to move through with patience
  • Confusing a planet's return to its own birth position (like a Saturn return) with gochara's house-by-house grading, which is a separate layer
  • Ignoring that different planets have entirely different favourable-house patterns — there is no single "good transit house" that applies to every planet
  • Applying Western transit-from-ascendant intuitions directly onto a Vedic chart without adjusting for the Moon-based counting

Where Traditions Differ

Western tropical astrology reads transits from the ascendant (and often from natal planet positions) rather than from the Moon, so a reader moving between the two systems will get genuinely different house counts for the same sky. Within Vedic astrology itself, schools also differ on how much weight to give gochara relative to dasha (planetary period) timing when the two seem to disagree — some treat dasha as the primary layer and gochara as a modifier, others read them as equally weighted.

Questions people ask

What is gochara in Vedic astrology?

Gochara means transit: the reading of where planets currently sit in the sky against your fixed natal chart. Unlike Western astrology, Vedic gochara is traditionally counted from your natal Moon sign rather than your ascendant, because the Moon is read as the mind moving through changing skies over time.

Why are Vedic transits counted from the Moon instead of the ascendant?

Classical Vedic technique treats the Moon as representing the mind, and gochara as the weather the mind moves through. Counting from the natal Moon keeps the transit reading tied to that idea, distinct from the Western convention of counting transits from the ascendant or from natal planetary positions.

What is sade sati and how long does it last?

Sade sati is Saturn's transit through the 12th, 1st and 2nd houses counted from your natal Moon, lasting about seven and a half years in total. It recurs roughly once every thirty years, as Saturn completes one orbit. Traditional readings describe it as a demanding but constructive season of pruning, not a guaranteed misfortune.

What is chandrashtama?

Chandrashtama is the transiting Moon's passage through the 8th house counted from your natal Moon, lasting about two days roughly every month. Tradition reads it as a low-energy stretch better suited to finishing existing tasks than starting new ones, passing on its own within a couple of days.

What is a Saturn return in Vedic astrology?

A Saturn return is Saturn transiting back to its exact natal position, which happens near ages 29 and a half, 59, and 88 and a half, since Saturn takes about 29.5 years to orbit the zodiac. Vedic tradition reads it as a season when existing structures are tested and reset for the next cycle.

What is tara bala?

Tara bala is a nine-category daily rating found by counting from your birth nakshatra to the Moon's current nakshatra and reducing that count to a repeating nine-fold cycle. It is a finer, faster layer than gochara, traditionally used for choosing the timing of smaller day-to-day decisions rather than reading long transits.

Does every planet have the same favourable transit houses?

No. Classical texts give each planet its own distinct set of favourable houses counted from the natal Moon — Jupiter's pattern differs from Saturn's, which differs again from Venus's. There is no single house that reads as universally good for every transiting planet; the grading has to be checked planet by planet.

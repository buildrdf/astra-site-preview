How to Read a Kundali: The Full Reasoning Chain

No single placement decides anything. A chart is read the way a case is argued.

The short answer

A real kundali reading walks one chain, link by link: a graha's exact placement gives its sign, which gives its house and lordship, refined by its nakshatra and dignity, then weighed against its aspects and conjunctions, and finally checked against dasha and transit timing. No single link decides a reading; several links pointing the same way build a strong conclusion, and disagreement means the honest answer is a mixed one.

One chain, used for every reading

By this point you've met the individual pieces of a Vedic birth chart — signs, houses, grahas, nakshatras, dashas. This is about the order they go in. Every reading a competent astrologer produces — for a house, a graha, a single day, or a multi-year period — is built by walking the same eight-link chain. Each link either adds weight to a conclusion or takes weight away, and the final reading is the sum of all of them, not any one link alone.

The eight links, in order

  • Placement — the graha's exact longitude; every link below is derived from this one number
  • Sign — which of the 12 rashis holds it, which fixes its element and its ruling lord
  • House — which of the 12 areas of life it acts on, counted from the ascendant (lagna)
  • Lordship — which houses that graha itself rules, and so what it carries into the chart
  • Nakshatra — the finer 13°20′ position, and which graha rules that nakshatra
  • Dignity — how comfortable the graha is in the sign it landed in (exalted, own sign, debilitated, and the shades between)
  • Conjunction and drishti (aspect) — which other grahas it shares a sign with, or looks toward
  • Dasha and transit — whether any of the above is currently "switched on" by timing

No individual link is decisive on its own. A chart is read the way a legal case is argued: several independent lines of evidence pointing the same direction build a strong, confident reading, and lines that pull in different directions mean the honest conclusion is a mixed or conditional one. Saying "this is mixed" is not a failure of the method — it's frequently the correct, most honest answer a chain like this can give.

A worked example: Saturn in Capricorn, 9th house

Take a chart with a Taurus ascendant, and Saturn sitting in Capricorn in the 9th house. Walking the chain link by link shows how a real reading accumulates.

  • Sign — Capricorn is one of Saturn's own signs, so it is at home: comfortable, and reliably itself. Weight added.
  • House — from a Taurus lagna, Capricorn falls in the 9th house: teachers, belief, the father, long journeys, and what classical texts call fortune.
  • Lordship — Saturn rules both Capricorn and Aquarius, which from a Taurus lagna are the 9th and 10th houses. A trikona (fortune house) and a kendra (angle) ruled by the same graha makes this Saturn what classical texts call a yogakaraka — a graha whose two house rulerships are read as unusually favorable together.
  • Nakshatra — between 10° and 23°20′ of Capricorn sits Shravana, whose ruling graha is the Moon and whose classical symbol is an ear. A Saturn of listening and learning, sitting in the house of teachers: the links agree, so the reading firms up rather than staying speculative.
  • Dignity — own sign, so there is nothing to correct for. Had this same Saturn instead sat in Aries, its sign of debilitation, the identical lordship story would traditionally be read as arriving more slowly or with more friction, even though the house and lordship facts wouldn't change at all.
  • Drishti (aspect) — from the 9th house, Saturn's classical aspects fall on the 3rd, 6th, and 11th houses (effort, rivals, and gains) — but not on the 10th, the house it actually rules. That's a specific, checkable detail no simple keyword lookup would surface.
  • Company (conjunction) — whichever graha shares Capricorn with this Saturn shifts its tone further, and a graha in a mutual sign exchange with it would bind those two houses' stories together even more tightly.

The link that decides when: dasha and transit

The last link in the chain settles whether everything above is actually live right now. A yogakaraka Saturn, in this example, is a standing promise built into the chart — but classical practice reads a graha's mahadasha (major period) or antardasha (sub-period) as when that promise is understood to actually pay out. Two people holding the identical placement — one thirty years from their Saturn period starting, one in the middle of it — carry the same chart on paper and live very different years in practice. Transits (gochara) apply the same logic on a much shorter clock, month to month rather than decade to decade.

Two further refinements: vargottama and shadbala

Vargottamaa graha that holds the same sign in both the main birth chart (rashi) and the navamsa (D9) chart — traditionally read as steadying whatever that graha touches

Shadbalaliterally "six-fold strength" — a composite numeric score built from a graha's position, time of birth, direction, motion, natural strength, and aspect

These two ideas sit on top of the same eight-link chain rather than replacing any part of it. Vargottama asks whether a graha repeats its sign in one of the sixteen classical divisional charts (the navamsa), which is traditionally read as adding stability to everything the chain above already says. Shadbala ranks the seven classical grahas by an overall numeric strength score, which is traditionally used to judge which of several competing significations in a chart is likely to speak loudest.

Why every step should be shown, not just the conclusion

An astrological conclusion you cannot trace back to a specific placement is a claim you have to take on faith — and Vedic astrology was never meant to work that way. A properly built reading names its evidence: which graha, which sign, which house, which nakshatra, which dignity, and which dasha made this particular sentence true. That transparency is what lets you personally agree or disagree with a reading, rather than simply accept or reject it wholesale.

You are allowed to disagree with any given reading. That is precisely what showing the chain is for. Classical traditions themselves disagree on a great deal of the finer detail — which is exactly why the underlying evidence matters more than any single verdict handed down without it.

Putting it together: a short checklist

  1. Find the graha's exact sign and degree
  2. Note which house that sign falls in from the ascendant
  3. Check what that graha itself rules, and where
  4. Find its nakshatra and that nakshatra's ruling graha
  5. Check its dignity — exalted, own sign, neutral, or debilitated
  6. List what it conjoins and what it aspects
  7. Check whether its dasha or a relevant transit is currently active
  8. Only then, weigh all of the above together into one reading

Pick any graha in your own chart and walk this same checklist by hand — sign, house, lordship, nakshatra, dignity, aspect, then timing. You'll be reasoning through it exactly the way a trained astrologer does, from placement to conclusion and back again. →

Questions people ask

What is the correct order to read a Vedic birth chart?

Start from a graha's exact placement, then its sign, the house that sign falls in from the ascendant, what that graha rules elsewhere in the chart, its nakshatra, its dignity, its conjunctions and aspects, and finally whether its dasha or a transit currently activates it. No single step is decisive alone — the reading is built from all of them together.

What is a yogakaraka in Vedic astrology?

A yogakaraka is a graha that rules both a kendra (angular house: 1, 4, 7, or 10) and a trikona (fortune house: 1, 5, or 9) from a given ascendant. Because it controls houses classical texts treat as especially favorable together, its placement is traditionally read as unusually significant in that chart.

What does vargottama mean?

Vargottama describes a graha that lands in the same sign in both the main birth chart (rashi) and the navamsa (D9) divisional chart. Classical texts read this repetition as adding stability and strength to whatever that graha already signifies, on top of its ordinary sign and house placement.

What is shadbala?

Shadbala, meaning six-fold strength, is a composite numeric score built from six factors — a graha's position, timing, directional strength, motion, natural strength, and aspect. It's traditionally used to compare the seven classical grahas against each other and judge which significations in a chart carry the most weight.

Why do two astrologers sometimes read the same chart differently?

Because several links in the reading chain — dignity, aspect, dasha timing, and especially which classical text or convention to lean on — leave room for informed judgment. Traditions genuinely differ on some of this. A well-built reading names its evidence so you can see exactly where two readers parted ways.

Do I need to know dashas to read a chart?

You need them to know when a placement is considered active, not to know what it means in general. A chart's sign, house, and lordship facts hold true for a lifetime; dasha and transit are the layer that says which of those standing facts are traditionally read as switched on right now.

What Are the 27 Nakshatras in Vedic Astrology?

A 27-part lunar map, older than the twelve signs, that tracks the Moon day by day.

The short answer

The 27 nakshatras are lunar mansions that divide the 360° zodiac into equal 13°20' segments, each with its own ruling graha, deity, and symbol. They track the Moon's roughly daily motion, refine the reading of the Moon sign, and — through the janma nakshatra holding the Moon at birth — set the starting point of the Vimshottari dasha timeline.

A Grid Older Than the Twelve Signs

Long before Indian astronomy adopted the twelve 30° zodiac signs, it divided the sky into 27 nakshatras, or lunar mansions, each exactly 13°20' wide. The number isn't arbitrary: the Moon travels roughly one nakshatra per day, so 27 of them complete a sidereal lunar month. Where a rashi (zodiac sign) takes the Moon about two and a quarter days to cross, a nakshatra takes about one — a finer, faster-moving grid laid directly under the signs.

Nakshatralunar mansion — one of 27 divisions of the zodiac, each 13°20' wide

Why Nakshatras Often Say More Than the Moon Sign Alone

Because each 30° sign holds a little over two nakshatras, two people can share the exact same Moon sign and still land in different nakshatras — with different ruling grahas, different presiding deities, and different Vimshottari dasha sequences. That's a meaningfully different reading hiding underneath what looks, at the rashi level, like an identical placement.

Padas: The Quarter Inside Each Nakshatra

Each nakshatra divides again into four padas of 3°20' each, always counted from the nakshatra's own starting point — never as a simple remainder of the Moon's longitude, since that can misplace a pada at the boundary. Four padas across 27 nakshatras give 108 divisions in total, the same number that recurs throughout Indian tradition (108 beads on a mala, 108 names in many stotras).

Padaquarter — one of the four 3°20' subdivisions of a nakshatra, counted from the nakshatra's own start

The pada matters practically: it decides which sign a graha lands in on the navamsa chart (the D9 divisional chart, covered at the Expert level), and it sharpens a nakshatra's broad theme into one of four narrower shades.

Nine Ruling Grahas, in a Fixed Repeating Cycle

Each nakshatra has one ruling graha, assigned in a fixed cycle of nine that begins with Ketu at Ashwini (the first nakshatra) and simply repeats: Ketu, Venus, Sun, Moon, Mars, Rahu, Jupiter, Saturn, Mercury — then back to Ketu at the tenth nakshatra, Magha. Because 27 divides evenly by 9, this cycle repeats exactly three times across the full belt.

  • The same nine-graha cycle that rules nakshatras also sets the order of the Vimshottari dasha sequence
  • Each nakshatra carries a traditional presiding deity and symbol, used as interpretive keywords rather than fixed predictions
  • The Moon's nakshatra at birth — the janma nakshatra — sets your first mahadasha and how much of it had already elapsed before you were born

All 27 Nakshatras: Span, Lord, Deity, and Symbol

Nine nakshatras — Krittika, Mrigashira, Punarvasu, Uttara Phalguni, Chitra, Vishakha, Uttara Ashadha, Dhanishta, and Purva Bhadrapada — straddle two signs. A Moon near a sign boundary needs a precise birth time to know which side of the nakshatra it actually falls on.

How to Find Your Own Nakshatra and Pada

  1. Get the Moon's exact sidereal longitude at your birth moment (Lahiri ayanamsa, Astra's default)
  2. Divide that longitude by 13°20' — the whole-number result (0 to 26) picks the nakshatra from the table above
  3. Take the remainder — how far the Moon sits past the nakshatra's own start — and divide by 3°20' to get the pada, 1 through 4
  4. Look up the ruling graha, deity, and symbol for that nakshatra from the table

Where Classical Sources Differ

Deity names and interpretive keywords vary slightly across classical texts and regional schools. The deity and symbol attributions above follow the widely used Parashari and Hora Sastra tradition; treat the keywords as traditional associations for reflection, not as fixed predictions about anyone born under them.

Common Mistakes With Nakshatras

  • Calculating a pada as a plain remainder of longitude modulo 3°20' instead of measuring from the nakshatra's own start — this misplaces boundary cases
  • Assuming a nakshatra maps neatly to one sign, when nine of the 27 straddle two signs
  • Treating the deity or symbol as a literal prediction rather than a traditional association
  • Forgetting that every graha, not just the Moon, has its own nakshatra and pada — 'my nakshatra' usually means the Moon's, but a full reading checks all nine

Questions people ask

How many nakshatras are there in Vedic astrology?

There are 27 nakshatras, each spanning exactly 13°20' of the 360° zodiac. They divide further into four padas of 3°20' each, giving 108 padas in total. The cycle of 27 nakshatras is older than the twelve-sign zodiac and was originally used to track the Moon's daily motion through the sky.

What is my nakshatra based on?

Your janma nakshatra (birth star) is based on the exact sidereal longitude of the Moon at the moment you were born, not your date alone. Because the Moon moves about 13° a day, an inaccurate birth time can place you in the wrong nakshatra, especially near a boundary.

What is a nakshatra pada?

A pada is one of the four equal 3°20' quarters within a nakshatra, counted from that nakshatra's own starting degree. It refines the nakshatra's broad theme into a narrower shade and determines which sign the point falls in on the navamsa (D9) divisional chart.

Which planet rules which nakshatra?

Nakshatra rulership follows a fixed cycle of nine grahas — Ketu, Venus, Sun, Moon, Mars, Rahu, Jupiter, Saturn, Mercury — starting at Ashwini and repeating three times across the 27 nakshatras. The same nine-graha order and proportions also govern the Vimshottari dasha sequence.

Can two people have the same Moon sign but different nakshatras?

Yes. Each 30° Moon sign contains a little over two nakshatras, so people who share a Moon sign frequently fall into different nakshatras, with different ruling grahas, deities, and dasha sequences — a meaningful difference hidden beneath an apparently identical sign.

Do nakshatras only apply to the Moon?

No. Every graha and the lagna each occupy their own nakshatra and pada, and a thorough chart reading checks all of them. In everyday speech, however, 'my nakshatra' almost always refers specifically to the Moon's nakshatra, since that is the one that sets the dasha timeline.

Retrograde and Combust Planets in Vedic Astrology, Explained

One is a trick of vantage point. The other is a trick of glare. Neither is a flaw in you.

The short answer

Retrograde (vakri) means a planet appears to move backward against the stars as seen from Earth, purely due to relative orbital speed — nothing physically reverses. Combust (asta) means a planet sits close enough to the Sun in longitude to be lost in its glare. They are independent conditions: a planet can be one, both, or neither at once.

Two States, Often Confused

Open any transit report and you may see a planet marked both R for retrograde and combust in the same line, and it is easy to assume these describe one thing. They do not. Retrograde is about direction of apparent motion against the stars. Combust is about closeness to the Sun in the sky. A planet can be retrograde and nowhere near the Sun, combust while moving perfectly forward, both together, or neither.

Retrograde (Vakri): An Illusion of Perspective

Vakriretrograde — the apparent backward motion of a graha (planet) against the background stars, as seen from Earth

No planet ever actually reverses course. Every planet, including Earth, orbits the Sun in the same direction. Retrograde motion is a trick of vantage: as Earth, moving faster on an inner orbit, overtakes a slower outer planet like Mars or Saturn, that planet briefly appears to drift backward against the zodiac before resuming forward motion — the same effect as a slower car seeming to slide backward when you overtake it on a highway.

  1. Take a planet's sidereal longitude today
  2. Take the same planet's longitude exactly one day later
  3. If the longitude has decreased, the planet reads as retrograde for that day; if it has increased, it reads as direct

The Sun and Moon are never retrograde — Earth's own motion around the Sun and the Moon's motion around Earth do not produce this backward illusion. Rahu and Ketu, the lunar nodes, are treated as permanently retrograde by convention, since their mean position always regresses through the zodiac over the long run.

The true (as opposed to mean) lunar node actually wobbles: measured day by day, its motion turns briefly forward for a few days roughly every fortnight. Classical texts, and Astra, hold to the older convention that the nodes are always retrograde, because that is how their long-run motion behaves and how every traditional source treats them.

What a Retrograde Planet Traditionally Means

Within Vedic tradition a retrograde graha is often read as strengthened but turned inward — its themes revisited, reworked and internalised rather than expressed in a straight line outward. A retrograde Mercury, for instance, is traditionally read as a season for revising, rereading and reconsidering rather than launching something new. That is a description of emphasis, not a warning: retrograde periods are common, temporary, and not traditionally read as misfortune.

Combust (Asta): Too Close to the Sun

Astacombust — a graha positioned too close to the Sun in longitude to be seen against the glare of daylight

A planet close enough to the Sun disappears from the visible sky long before conjunction — it rises and sets so near the Sun that its own light is lost in the glare. Tradition calls this combustion and reads the planet's expression as dimmed for a while: present in the chart, but harder to see, in the sky and, by extension, in the life the chart describes.

The Classical Combustion Orbs

Combustion is measured as angular separation between a planet's longitude and the Sun's longitude. Each planet has its own traditional orb, inside which it is read as combust. A retrograde inner planet (Mercury or Venus) sits physically between Earth and the Sun, so tradition tightens its orb slightly when retrograde:

The Sun itself is never combust, since combustion is measured from the Sun. Rahu and Ketu are shadow points rather than physical bodies with their own light to lose, so combustion is not traditionally read for them.

Worked Example: A Retrograde, Combust Mercury

Suppose the Sun sits at 20° Virgo and Mercury, moving retrograde, sits at 27° Virgo — a separation of 7°. Because Mercury is retrograde, its combustion orb tightens from 14° to 12°. Seven degrees is inside that 12° orb, so this Mercury reads as both retrograde and combust at once: its usual themes of communication and analysis are read as turned inward by the retrograde and dimmed by the combustion — a double, temporary muting, not a double misfortune.

Check your own free chart for any planet within its combustion orb of the Sun, and note separately whether it is also marked retrograde. They rarely mean the same thing even when they land on the same planet. →

Common Mistakes About Retrograde and Combust Planets

  • Treating retrograde as universally unlucky — it is a direction of apparent motion, not a verdict
  • Assuming the Sun or Moon can be retrograde — by definition, neither ever is
  • Reading combustion and retrogression as the same condition, when a planet can have either without the other
  • Using one flat combustion orb for every planet, when the classical orbs differ meaningfully by planet
  • Forgetting that these are temporary transit conditions, not permanent marks on a birth chart's promise

Where Classical Texts Differ

Not every school applies solar combustion to the Moon at all, since a Moon close to the Sun is simply the new-moon (amavasya) phase, a monthly and expected event rather than an anomaly. Astra follows the widely used Parashari orb of 12° for the Moon as well, and states this plainly since it is a genuine point of disagreement rather than settled consensus.

Texts also disagree on how much a retrograde planet's strength actually changes: some hold that a retrograde graha gives fuller results, comparable to being in its own sign, while others read it more cautiously as simply redirected rather than strengthened. Where sources disagree this openly, the honest position is to name the disagreement rather than pick a side and print it as settled fact.

Questions people ask

What does it mean when a planet is retrograde in Vedic astrology?

Retrograde (vakri) means the planet appears to move backward through the zodiac as seen from Earth, caused purely by the relative speeds of Earth's and that planet's orbits around the Sun. Nothing physically reverses. Vedic tradition often reads a retrograde planet as strengthened but turned inward, its themes revisited rather than launched fresh.

Can the Sun or Moon be retrograde?

No. Retrograde motion is an apparent backward drift caused by comparing one planet's orbital motion to Earth's. Because the Sun's motion is Earth's own orbit viewed from Earth, and the Moon orbits Earth directly, neither ever produces this backward illusion. Both are always read as moving direct.

What does combust mean in astrology?

Combust (asta) describes a planet positioned close enough to the Sun in longitude that its light is lost in the Sun's glare, both in the physical sky and in the chart reading. Each planet has its own traditional combustion orb; a planet inside that orb is read as combust, its usual significations dimmed rather than absent.

What is the combustion orb for Mercury and Venus?

Mercury's classical combustion orb is about 14° when moving direct and 12° when retrograde. Venus is about 10° direct and 8° retrograde. The orb tightens when retrograde because both planets orbit closer to the Sun than Earth does, so a retrograde Mercury or Venus sits physically between Earth and the Sun.

Are Rahu and Ketu always retrograde?

Yes, by long-standing convention. Rahu and Ketu are the Moon's nodes, mathematical points rather than physical bodies, and their mean position always regresses through the zodiac over time. Measured day to day the true node briefly moves forward at times, but classical texts and most software hold the nodes to be permanently retrograde.

Is a retrograde or combust planet a bad sign in a birth chart?

Neither condition is traditionally read as misfortune. Retrograde suggests a planet's themes are internalised and revisited rather than expressed outwardly; combust suggests those themes are harder to see for a while. Both are common, temporary or cyclical states, and classical texts describe them as different rather than damaging.

Does every school of Vedic astrology use the same combustion rules?

No. While the numeric orbs above are widely used, some schools do not apply solar combustion to the Moon at all, treating a Moon near the Sun as simply the new-moon phase. Traditions also differ on how much a retrograde planet's strength actually changes. These are genuine, unresolved points of disagreement, not errors in either camp.

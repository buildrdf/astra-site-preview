Lahiri Ayanamsa: Sidereal vs Tropical Zodiac Explained

Two zodiacs that once agreed, and the slow wobble that pulled them apart.

The short answer

The tropical zodiac (Western astrology) anchors 0° Aries to the spring equinox; the sidereal zodiac (Vedic astrology) anchors it to the fixed stars. Earth's slow axial wobble has pulled the two apart by the ayanamsa, currently a little over 24° using the Lahiri ayanamsa this app computes, and growing by about 50.3 arcseconds a year.

Two zodiacs, two starting lines

Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac: 0° Aries is defined as the moment of the spring equinox, the point where the Sun crosses the celestial equator each year. Vedic astrology uses the sidereal zodiac: 0° Aries is anchored instead to the fixed background of stars. Roughly two thousand years ago the two zodiacs lined up almost exactly. They have been drifting apart ever since, and today they no longer agree.

Why they drift: the wobble

Precession of the equinoxes

Earth doesn't just spin — its axis also traces a slow circle in space, the way a spinning top's axis wanders, completing one full turn in roughly 26,000 years. Astronomers call this the precession of the equinoxes. It drags the equinox point slowly backward through the fixed constellations, at a rate of about one degree every 72 years. The tropical zodiac moves with the seasons and the equinox; the sidereal zodiac stays fixed against the stars. The accumulating gap between them is the ayanamsa.

Ayanamsathe angular gap, in degrees, between the tropical and sidereal zodiacs at a given moment

Because the gap grows continuously, ayanamsa is not a single fixed number — it is a slowly increasing value that any serious sidereal calculation must compute for the specific date in question, not read off a table frozen years ago.

How big is the gap right now?

This app computes ayanamsa using the Lahiri formula for the exact date of your birth chart. As of now, the value sits at a little over 24° and continues to grow at roughly 50.3 arcseconds per year — about a fortieth of a degree annually, or one full degree roughly every 72 years, consistent with the precession rate above.

Why your Vedic sign is usually one back from your Western one

Since the current ayanamsa is a little over 24° — most of the way through a 30° sign — most people's sidereal sign sits one sign behind their tropical one. A lifelong Western Leo is, very often, a Vedic Cancer. This is not a mistake in either system and it is not evidence that one zodiac is "more accurate" than the other: tropical and sidereal astrology are simply measuring from different reference points, each internally consistent on its own terms, and asking a different question about the sky.

There is no single "my sign" independent of which zodiac you mean. A tropical Leo and a sidereal Cancer can be the same person, the same birth moment, and the same underlying astronomy — just two different conventions for slicing the same circle of stars into twelve.

Which ayanamsa does this app use, and why

Pinning down the exact gap requires choosing a specific historical moment when the two zodiacs are considered to have coincided exactly — and different traditions choose differently. The Lahiri ayanamsa, also known as the Chitrapaksha ayanamsa, was adopted by the Indian government's calendar reform committee in the 1950s and is the most widely used standard in Vedic astrology today. It is what this app uses throughout.

Other ayanamsas: Raman, Krishnamurti, and the rest

Lahiri is not the only ayanamsa in use. The Raman ayanamsa and the Krishnamurti (KP) ayanamsa are two other commonly cited alternatives, and each differs from Lahiri by a small fraction of a degree — usually well under a full degree, but enough, in a chart where a graha sits close to a sign or nakshatra boundary, to shift that graha's sign, house, or nakshatra assignment entirely. This is exactly why any serious astrology software should state which ayanamsa it uses: without that, a printed chart position cannot be fully trusted or reproduced.

Sidereal, tropical, and the rest of the chart

The choice of zodiac affects every graha's sign and nakshatra placement, and everything built on top of those — house lordship, dignity, dasha calculations tied to the Moon's nakshatra, and more. It does not change the underlying astronomical positions of the planets themselves, only which 30° slice of sky each position is said to fall into. Two pieces of software computing the same birth moment in the same zodiac, with the same ayanamsa, should agree on sign placements to the degree; disagreement almost always traces back to a different ayanamsa choice, not an error in either engine.

A worked example

Say a tropical (Western) calculation places the Sun at 8° Leo for a given birth moment. Subtracting the current Lahiri ayanamsa — a little over 24° — moves that same, physically identical Sun back into sidereal Cancer, at roughly 14° Cancer. Nothing about the Sun's actual position in the sky changed between the two numbers; only the ruler used to mark off "zero" moved. This is the entire mechanism behind why a lifelong Western Leo so often turns out to be a Vedic Cancer.

A common mistake: treating the ayanamsa as fixed

A frequent error, including in some casual software, is hard-coding a single ayanamsa value ("24 degrees") rather than computing it fresh for the exact birth date. Because the value grows by about 50.3 arcseconds a year, a chart calculated this way for a birth 40 years ago could already be off by roughly half a degree — enough, near a sign boundary, to shift a graha into the wrong sign entirely.

The correct approach, and the one this app follows, is to treat ayanamsa as a function of time and evaluate it at the exact birth date, not to look up one number and reuse it for every chart. This matters most for anyone born decades ago, or for anyone comparing an old printed chart against a freshly generated one — small ayanamsa drift, compounded over years, is one of the most common reasons two otherwise correct calculations disagree.

Where practitioners genuinely differ

Beyond the Lahiri, Raman, and Krishnamurti ayanamsas already mentioned, a handful of other sidereal reference points exist in scholarly and practitioner circles, each anchored to a different historical or astronomical marker. None of these disagreements are errors — they represent real, long-standing differences in which moment of history a tradition treats as the point where tropical and sidereal zodiacs coincided. What matters for a reader comparing charts across sources is simply knowing which convention is in play, since the underlying astronomy behind all of them is identical.

Questions people ask

What is the current value of the Lahiri ayanamsa?

The Lahiri ayanamsa currently sits at a little over 24° and grows by about 50.3 arcseconds — roughly a fortieth of a degree — every year, as Earth's axis slowly precesses. Because it changes continuously, accurate sidereal software computes it fresh for each birth date rather than using one fixed figure.

Why is my Western sign different from my Vedic sign?

Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac, anchored to the spring equinox; Vedic astrology uses the sidereal zodiac, anchored to the fixed stars. The two have drifted apart by the ayanamsa, currently just over 24°, which is why most people's Vedic sign sits one sign behind the Western sign they may already know.

Is the sidereal zodiac more accurate than the tropical zodiac?

Neither is more astronomically "correct" — they measure from different reference points on purpose. Tropical tracks the seasons and equinox; sidereal tracks the fixed stars. Vedic astrology has always used the sidereal zodiac, which is why this app computes chart positions sidereally, using the Lahiri ayanamsa.

What is the difference between Lahiri and Raman ayanamsa?

Both are sidereal ayanamsas, but they choose a different historical reference moment for where the two zodiacs coincided, so their values differ by a small fraction of a degree. That difference can shift a graha near a sign boundary into a different sign or nakshatra, which is why matching software should always state which ayanamsa it uses.

Does the ayanamsa ever stop growing?

No — it grows continuously as long as Earth's axis precesses, which is an ongoing astronomical process on a roughly 26,000-year cycle. The rate is nearly constant on human timescales, at about 50.3 arcseconds a year, so the ayanamsa will keep increasing for millennia.

How does the ayanamsa affect my birth chart?

It shifts every graha's sidereal sign and nakshatra placement relative to the tropical positions you might see in Western astrology, and everything derived from those — house lordship, dignity, and nakshatra-based dasha timing. It does not change the planets' actual astronomical positions, only which sidereal slice of sky each one is assigned to.
